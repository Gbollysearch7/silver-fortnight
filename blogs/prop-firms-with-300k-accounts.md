# Prop Firms With 300k Accounts: How Traders Get There

A $300k funded account is the ceiling at most retail prop firms, and there are only two ways to reach it: buy the biggest challenge a firm sells, or scale a smaller account up by trading it well. Most traders searching for **prop firms with 300k accounts** assume buying big is the smart shortcut. Usually it just makes failure more expensive.

This guide covers both routes, what a 300k allocation actually is in a simulated funding model, the payout and drawdown maths that matter more than the headline number, and the red flags around firms waving huge allocations with no scaling logic behind them.

One reference point up front: at TradersYard the funding cap is **$300k in total allocation or 2 funded accounts, whichever comes first**. Keep that structure in mind, because it is how serious firms think about maximum allocation.

## The Two Routes to a 300k Funded Account

Route one is buying size outright. Many prop firms sell large evaluations at the top of their pricing page. You pay the biggest fee on offer, pass the same rules at bigger dollar values, and hold a large allocation from day one. Fast on paper. Expensive in practice, for reasons we will get to.

Route two is scaling. You pass a smaller, cheaper evaluation, then grow your allocation through the firm's published scaling plan as you deliver consistent results. TradersYard documents exactly how [account scaling](https://docs.tradersyard.com/traderchallenge/account-scaling) works, and there are **no time limits** on challenges or funded accounts. The timeline is set by your trading, not by a countdown clock.

Both routes end at the same place, because nearly every credible firm enforces an aggregate cap. The question is not whether you can hold 300k. It is how much you are willing to pay to find out whether you can trade it.

## Why Scaling Beats Buying the Biggest Challenge

Scaling wins for most traders for one blunt reason: **failure is cheaper**. An entry-level evaluation costs a fraction of a maximum-size one, and evaluations get failed. That is the nature of the product. Blow a small challenge and you retry for a small fee. At TradersYard, a failed account also comes with a 10% discount coupon on the next challenge. Blow a max-size challenge and you have burned the equivalent of several retries in one afternoon.

The second reason is proof of process. A trader who scaled up has weeks or months of rule-compliant trading behind them: drawdown respected, consistency maintained, payouts requested and received. Every allocation increase was earned against published criteria. Buying a 300k challenge outright tests nothing except your card limit.

Third, psychology. A routine red day on a 300k account is a four-figure number. If the largest account you have ever traded is your own $2,000, that number will change your behaviour, usually for the worse. Scaling lets the dollar swings grow at the same pace as your tolerance for them.

Build the route deliberately: a proper [funded trader scaling plan strategy](https://tradersyard.com/blog-posts/funded-trader-scaling-plan-strategy-how-to-grow-your-account) paired with a [scaling prop trading accounts checklist](https://tradersyard.com/blog-posts/scaling-prop-trading-accounts-checklist) will do more for your odds than any amount of upfront spend.

| Upfront cost | Highest fee on the pricing page | Entry-level fee |
| Cost of failure | One large fee gone | Small fee, cheap retry |
| Proof of process | None, you start at max size | Built in, every increase is earned |

## What 300k Actually Means in a Simulated Funding Model

Now the honest part, which too many firms mumble through. At TradersYard, and at most modern prop firms, the 300k is **virtual capital in a simulated environment**. Nobody hands you $300,000 in cash. Every TradersYard account is a demo account with virtual funds.

After you pass the Funded Level, you sign a Signal-Provider Contract. You provide trade signals, and TradersYard may copy them to its own corporate account. You never trade real client money and you are never liable for losses.

So what is the 300k, really? It is the number that sets your position sizing, your drawdown in dollars, and your **payout base**. Profit generated on that virtual balance converts into real money through the firm's split. The payouts are the real part, and the only part that should drive your decision.

Any firm implying you will be handed a live nine-figure book is selling a fantasy. If the model is still fuzzy, start with [what a prop firm is](https://tradersyard.com/blog-posts/what-is-a-prop-firm) and judge every allocation headline through that lens.

## The Maths That Matter More Than the Headline Number

**Split tiers first.** TradersYard runs a scalable split: the first $300 of profit is 100% yours, profit from $300 to $1,000 pays 90%, and everything above $1,000 pays 80%. A $1,200 profit works out to a $1,090 payout. A $6,000 profit works out to $4,930. The bigger your account, the more of your profit sits in the 80% tier, so model your expected take on realistic profit figures, not on the account size printed in the ad.

**Drawdown in dollars second.** Percentages feel abstract until you convert them. A fixed-percentage daily loss limit turns into very different dollar numbers depending on account size: modest on a 50k account, a lot heavier on a 300k account. Same percentage, completely different emotional event. Know your [drawdown](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/d/drawdown.asp) type before you size up. TradersYard publishes three: daily (equity-based, resets 00:00 UTC), static (fixed), and end-of-day max, which only trails upward.

**Consistency rules third.** TradersYard's 40% consistency rule says your best single day can be no more than 40% of your total closed profit. At 300k, one oversized winner can leave your best day above that threshold until steadier days dilute it. Large accounts reward steady extraction, not hero days.

For the full mechanics of minimums, cycles and processing times, read our guide to [prop firm payouts, profit splits and withdrawals](https://tradersyard.com/blog-posts/prop-firm-payouts-profit-split-withdrawals).

## Why Firms Cap Maximum Allocation

Every credible prop firm caps aggregate allocation, and the reason is simple: in a signal-provider model, the firm may mirror your trades with its own money. Its real-world exposure has to stay bounded. A cap is not stinginess. It is evidence that the firm actually carries risk on your signals and has done the arithmetic.

TradersYard's cap is up to **$300k total or 2 funded accounts, whichever comes first**, with a $100k cap for traders in Malaysia, Pakistan and Indonesia. Those numbers are published before you buy, which is exactly where you want to see them.

Now flip the logic. A firm advertising uncapped or seven-figure allocations to anyone with a card is telling you one of two things. Either it never intends to put meaningful size behind your signals, or it has not done the risk maths. Neither ends well for your payout.

## Red Flags When a Firm Advertises Huge Allocations

Some firms lead with enormous account sizes and hope you never ask how the machinery works. Before you pay, check for these:

- **No published scaling rules.** If a giant allocation exists but the path to it is not documented anywhere official, the number is marketing, not capital.
- **No disclosed aggregate cap.** Unlimited allocation implies unlimited firm exposure, which no risk desk on earth signs off on.
- **Terms that deteriorate at size.** Splits that quietly drop, new fees, or payout throttles that only appear once you are holding a large account.
- **Rules that live in Discord replies** instead of official documentation. If the answer changes depending on which moderator you ask, it is not a rule.
- **Time pressure at scale.** Forced deadlines on large accounts push oversized trades. TradersYard runs no time limits for a reason.

The test is boring but reliable: can the firm's own documentation explain how allocation, drawdown, split and payout interact at maximum size? If yes, the 300k is a real ceiling you can work toward. If no, it is a billboard.
